Meet the Acidithiobacillia

The Class

Pioneers of extreme chemistry

The Acidithiobacillia are the dominant taxon in acid mine drainage and volcanic acidic systems — the very environments that define our lab's model ecosystem. For over a decade, Quatrini Lab has worked to understand how these organisms survive, diversify, and thrive where pH can fall below 1 and temperatures exceed 80 degrees Celsius.

Through phylogenomics, comparative genomics, metagenomics, and experimental evolution, we have traced the deep evolutionary history of this class, described new genera, and catalogued the genomic machinery that underpins their adaptation. Each species carries a distinct ecological signature — a unique combination of metabolic capacity, tolerance range, and genomic defense architecture.

Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans

The founding member of the class — an obligate chemolithotroph that oxidizes reduced sulfur compounds as its sole energy source. Remarkably acid-tolerant, capable of growth at pH below 1. A key driver of sulfuric acid generation in AMD systems and a central focus of our comparative genomics work, where its pangenome reveals both a conserved sulfur-metabolism core and a highly plastic genomic island compartment carrying defense and adaptive functions.

Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans

A metabolic generalist able to oxidize both ferrous iron and reduced sulfur, giving it a competitive edge across AMD niches. Its genome encodes one of the richest arsenals of restriction-modification systems described in acidophiles, reflecting intense phage pressure in community contexts. Our MOBHunter platform identified extensive mobile element content in this species, underlining the role of horizontal gene transfer in shaping its iron-oxidation gene clusters.

Acidithiobacillus caldus

The thermophilic outlier of the Acidithiobacillales — thriving at temperatures that challenge most sulfur oxidizers. Its elevated GC content is a thermodynamic adaptation, stabilizing nucleic acids under heat stress. In our experimental evolution studies, A. caldus populations under chloride stress revealed rapid adaptive trajectories, making it a key model for studying ion tolerance and selection pressure in industrial bioleaching environments.

Igneacidithiobacillus copahuensis

First described by Quatrini Lab in 2024, this organism originates from the Copahue volcanic system — a site within the Pacific Ring of Fire. Its deep phylogenetic divergence from classical Acidithiobacillus warranted the description of an entirely new genus. Phylogenomic analysis placed it alongside other isolates from tectonically active sites worldwide, suggesting volcanic geochemistry as a cradle for diversification within the Acidithiobacillia.

Acidithiobacillus ferridurans

Closely related to A. ferrooxidans but distinguished by subtler metabolic and genomic characters. A prime example of how fine-scale comparative genomics — a hallmark of our lab's approach — can resolve cryptic diversity within AMD communities. Its pangenome analysis reveals a relatively closed architecture with fewer genomic islands, contrasting with the open pangenome of its sibling species.

Acidiferrobacter thiooxydans

The defining species of the Acidiferrobacterales — the second order within the class and phylogenetically distant from classical Acidithiobacillus. Its broader pH and temperature tolerance reflects a more generalist niche. Metagenome-resolved genomics from AMD communities frequently recovers MAGs belonging to this clade, confirming its ecological relevance in community succession studies carried out by our group in Patagonian volcanic rivers.

Acidithiobacillus sulfuriphilus

A psychrotolerant sulfur oxidizer recovered from cold acidic environments. Its ability to function at near-freezing temperatures expands the known thermal envelope of the class. Comparative analysis within our group revealed cold-adaptation signatures in its genome — altered membrane lipid biosynthesis genes and cold-shock protein repertoires — contrasting sharply with the thermophilic clade represented by A. caldus.
